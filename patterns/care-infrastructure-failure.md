# Pattern: Care Infrastructure Failure

## Summary
Care-based systems collapse not because their design was wrong but because the material conditions that would make them work have been systematically destroyed.

## Core Dynamic
Care systems require care capacity — the time, relational stability, material security, and emotional resources needed for sustained care work. Those conditions are actively depleted by the same material and economic arrangements that generate demand for care. Precarity increases the need for social workers while making social work conditions untenable. Climate shocks generate demand for mutual aid while exhausting the networks that provide it. Each care provider's individual departure looks like a personal decision; the structural cause — that the system extracts care capacity without replenishing it — is visible only in aggregate and only after the infrastructure has already failed. The system then responds by demanding more care from the remaining providers.

## Signals
- Mutual aid networks sustain high output during the acute phase of a crisis and collapse in the weeks after, as volunteers burn out
- Care worker caseloads exceed levels compatible with effective care; workers report going through the motions
- Care organizations rely structurally on volunteer labor for functions that require sustained, skilled presence
- Funding cuts to care infrastructure are accompanied by rhetoric about community responsibility and informal support networks
- Care workers exit the profession at accelerating rates, citing conditions rather than lack of commitment
- Communities most in need of care infrastructure have the fewest care providers, because material conditions have driven providers elsewhere
- Crisis events generate outpourings of care that cannot be sustained once the media cycle moves on

## What It Targets
The relational substrate of collective agency. When care capacity is depleted, the conditions that make coordinated action possible — sustained relationships, reliable support, the capacity to show up over time — dissolve. The result is not just worse services but reduced capacity for collective governance.

## Why It Works
Depletion is gradual and individualized. Each burnout event appears as a personal failure or a staffing problem, not as a structural signal. The solution — resourcing care as infrastructure — is consistently framed as a cost and blocked by the same political arrangements that benefit from keeping people isolated and exhausted. And the care ethic itself is weaponized: those who raise the structural problem are told to be more resilient, more committed, or more grateful.

## Common Misreadings
- "People are burning out because they're not setting good boundaries"
- "The community needs to step up and support each other"
- "This is a funding problem, not a governance problem"
- "We need to train more care workers"
- "The volunteers were amazing — it just wasn't sustainable"
