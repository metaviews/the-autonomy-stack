# Module: Commons & Institutional Governance

*Autonomy Stack — Stage 2 Module*
*Last updated: 2026-03-29*

---

## Core Argument

Commons governance is not the past. It is the architecture that existing power structures were built by destroying, and it is the design target for those who want governance that is accountable to the people it serves without reproducing the failures of state provision or private extraction.

The commons is not a nostalgic form — a reference to pre-industrial village life, to be admired and mourned. It is a living governance architecture: shared resources managed by their users through explicit rules, contested benefit distribution, and mechanisms for adaptation that do not require either the state to command or the market to decide. It was the dominant governance form in many societies before systematic enclosure, and the historical evidence is that it was resilient, adaptive, and capable of managing complex shared resources over centuries.

What the commons is not: identical to state provision. A public utility is not a commons unless its users govern it. Medicare is not a commons. The difference is not ownership but governance: who sets the rules, who distributes the benefit, who has standing to contest decisions, who is accountable when things go wrong. State provision can guarantee access without providing governance participation. A commons requires both.

What the commons is also not: infinitely scalable or universally applicable. It works best at scales where participants can hold each other accountable, where the resource can be bounded and monitored, and where the community has sufficient shared interest to sustain collective governance. The design challenge is not to make everything a commons but to build commons governance where it is appropriate and to create explicit institutional relationships between commons, state, and market where they must coexist.

This module develops governance architecture for building commons and institutions that remain genuinely accountable to the people they serve — addressing the three failure modes, the transition problem, and the scale problem directly.

## What Commons Governance Is

Elinor Ostrom's foundational research documented that common-pool resources — shared fisheries, irrigation systems, forests, grazing lands — were frequently governed successfully by user communities over long periods without the tragedy of the commons that economic theory predicted. The conditions that made successful commons governance possible are now well-documented:

**Clearly defined boundaries.** Who is a community member and who is not. What resources are included and what are excluded. Without boundaries, the community cannot identify who has rights, responsibilities, and standing.

**Congruence with local conditions.** Rules governing the resource match the actual characteristics of the resource and the needs of the community. Generic rules imposed from outside fail because they don't fit the specific conditions.

**Collective choice arrangements.** The people most affected by the rules have standing to modify them. Rules that cannot be contested by those they govern are not commons governance; they are imposed governance.

**Monitoring.** Resource conditions and user behavior are observed — by the users themselves or by accountable monitors. Without monitoring, rules cannot be enforced and free-riding cannot be detected.

**Graduated sanctions.** Violations are responded to proportionately. First offenses generate warnings and correction; persistent violations generate escalating consequences. This is different from both zero-tolerance enforcement (which destroys trust) and non-enforcement (which destroys the commons).

**Conflict resolution.** Low-cost, accessible mechanisms for resolving disputes between users and between users and governance bodies. The commons generates conflict; what matters is whether conflict is resolved in ways that maintain the community or fracture it.

**Recognition of rights to organize.** External authorities — states, corporations, other governance bodies — recognize the community's right to govern itself. External override of commons governance is one of the primary vectors of commons destruction.

For complex modern commons — shared data infrastructure, urban green space, community healthcare systems, mutual aid networks — these principles apply with necessary adaptations. The boundaries may be membership criteria rather than geographic lines. The monitoring may be digital rather than direct observation. The conflict resolution may be mediated rather than face-to-face. But the underlying governance logic is the same: the people who depend on the resource govern it, the rules can be contested by those they affect, and violations are responded to proportionately and transparently.

## Historical Context: State Origin Through Destruction of Commons

The commons was not superseded by more efficient forms. It was deliberately destroyed.

The history of European state formation, colonial expansion, and capitalist development is substantially a history of enclosure: the conversion of shared land, water, forests, and fishing grounds into private property, backed by law and enforced through violence. Silvia Federici's work documents that this was not an incidental feature of modernization but a deliberate mechanism of control — breaking the bonds of mutual care that made collective resistance possible, creating dependency on wages and markets by destroying the subsistence commons that had provided material security outside market relations.

The contemporary relevance: when commons are built today, they are built against this history and in conditions where enclosure remains active. The platform economy is enclosure of digital commons. The privatization of municipal water systems is enclosure of water commons. The intellectual property regime is enclosure of the knowledge commons. Every commons built today is built in the context of ongoing suppression — by states that do not recognize the right to organize, by markets that can outcompete commons on price in the short term while externalizing the costs that make short-term cheapness possible, and by political actors who find commons governance threatening precisely because it provides material security and relational capacity outside their control.

This history is not background context. It shapes what commons governance requires: not just good design principles but explicit protection mechanisms, recognition of rights to organize, and the political capacity to resist enclosure when it comes. Building a commons without attending to the forces that will try to destroy it is building without foundations.

## Three Failure Modes

**Enclosure.** The conversion of a commons to private property or state monopoly. The classic failure mode: a commons that has successfully governed a shared resource becomes attractive precisely because it has worked, and external actors move to capture the resource (or its governance) for private or state extraction. Agricultural land trusts that succeed in building community farming capacity become targets for developers. Open-source software that becomes critical infrastructure becomes a target for corporate capture of its governance and standardization. Data commons that demonstrate value become targets for platform enclosure.

Design response: explicit anti-enclosure provisions in governance structures; legal forms (land trusts, cooperative law, commons licensing) that make enclosure structurally difficult; political capacity to contest enclosure when it arrives.

**Capture.** The governance of a commons is taken over by a subset of its users — typically the most resourced, organized, or politically connected — who then direct the commons for their benefit rather than for the whole community. Capture does not require formal ownership change; it requires control of decision-making processes. A cooperative whose board is dominated by its largest members is captured. A public institution whose appointments are controlled by a single interest group is captured. A commons whose meeting attendance is systematically higher among some user groups than others is drifting toward capture.

Design response: explicit diversity requirements in governance participation; term limits and rotation for governance roles; transparency about who holds what authority and on what terms; mechanisms for underrepresented users to contest decisions that affect them.

**Substitution.** A commons is replaced by state provision or market provision without equivalent accountability — often with the argument that the commons is inefficient, that it cannot scale, or that state resources are needed for a more systematic response. The displacement of mutual aid networks by NGOs that adopt their functions while discarding their governance model is the canonical example. The mutual aid network was accountable to its community; the NGO is accountable to its funders.

Design response: clarity about what commons governance provides that state or market provision does not — not just resource access but governance participation. Resist substitution that maintains the resource while dissolving the governance. Build explicit governance requirements into funding relationships so that state or market resources do not come with strings that capture governance.

## Active Construction, Not Defensive Protection

The most important shift in the recent corpus is the move from defending existing commons to building new ones. The two postures require different governance architectures.

Defensive commons governance is reactive: responding to enclosure attempts, documenting rights, mobilizing the existing community to resist external pressure. It assumes the commons already exists and is under threat. This posture is necessary but insufficient.

Active construction starts from different conditions: there is no commons yet, or what exists is inadequate for the scale of need. Building requires creating governance structures before the community is fully formed, acquiring or preserving the resource before it has been enclosed, and developing the relational infrastructure — trust, shared knowledge, conflict resolution capacity — that makes collective governance possible.

The community resource pooling model from the Canadian Indigenous farming context illustrates what active construction looks like under capital barriers. Individual entry into capital-intensive sectors (agriculture, housing, energy) is blocked by the scale of capital required. The commons response: pool land, capital, and labor across a community to achieve entry that no individual can manage alone. The governance challenge is explicit from the start: how are pooling decisions made? Who has standing? How are benefits distributed? How are exit rights handled without breaking the pool?

The design insight: the governance architecture must be built before the resource is secured, or it will be improvised under pressure and reproduce existing power structures. Communities that pool capital without explicit governance find that informal power — prior relationships, charismatic individuals, differential contribution levels — shapes outcomes in ways that benefit the best-positioned. Explicit governance is not bureaucracy; it is protection against informal capture.

**The "public has ended" posture.** The corpus surfaces a framing that merits direct engagement: "if the public has ended, what comes next?" This posture treats the current moment as post-institutional — state provision has failed, public agencies have been captured, and the question is not how to fix them but how to build the successor governance forms. It generates urgency and creativity that defensive postures cannot.

Its risk: a posture that treats all state institutions as failed slides into a posture that refuses to use state resources, legal protections, or formal rights — which leaves commons-building without the material support and legal cover that makes it viable. The response is not to celebrate state provision as sufficient but to use available state tools while building governance that does not depend on state benevolence. The distinction between using state resources and relying on state legitimacy is important.

## Transition Mechanics

The hardest question across all five modules is transition: how does governance actually change from the current arrangement to the one the module describes? This is where governance frameworks most often fail — the diagnosis is correct, the design is reasonable, and the implementation path is absent or wishful.

The commons module has more to say about this than most, because the corpus includes cases of actual transition:

**The First Nations farming case.** The transition from settler individual ownership toward collective Indigenous farming does not require displacing existing farmers. It exploits an existing structural condition — aging settler farmers without successors — and builds parallel governance capacity (collective land pooling, training infrastructure, market access) that can absorb the transition without confrontation. The politics of reconciliation and the economics of agricultural succession converge. The transition path is demographic, not revolutionary.

**The mutual aid to institution transition.** The movement from crisis mutual aid (informal, fast, relational) to ongoing care infrastructure (formal, sustained, accountable) is a transition that the Care module identifies as consistently underdesigned. The commons module's contribution: this transition requires an explicit governance moment — a collective decision to formalize, to write the rules, to create the accountability structures that informal trust cannot sustain at scale. That moment is often resisted because formalization feels like bureaucratization. The response: formalization that preserves governance participation rather than concentrating it in an organizational leadership.

**The cooperative conversion.** The transition of an existing private business to cooperative ownership is a well-documented path with established legal forms. It requires willing sellers (or structured acquisition), willing workers (who must govern what they acquire), and capital (which is the primary barrier). The governance lesson from successful conversions: the process of negotiating the transition IS the first act of cooperative governance. The community that cannot make collective decisions about acquisition cannot make collective decisions about management.

What remains most open: transitions that require displacing concentrated private power. When the resource being governed is currently held by an actor with the resources and political capacity to resist transition, the governance design that works under cooperative conditions does not apply. Transition mechanics that address incumbent resistance are the highest-leverage remaining gap across all modules.

## Institutional Forms

**Cooperative.** Worker-owned, consumer-owned, or multi-stakeholder cooperatives distribute governance rights to members. The governance model is established, legally supported in most jurisdictions, and has extensive case evidence. The challenge is capital: cooperatives are harder to capitalize than investor-owned firms because they cannot offer equity upside. Community share models, patient capital, and cooperative development funds are partial responses.

**Land trust.** Community land trusts remove land from speculative market by holding it in perpetual community ownership while allowing permanent use rights. Established primarily in housing; applicable to agricultural land, urban green space, and community facilities. The governance challenge: land trusts require competent governance bodies that maintain their mandate across leadership transitions. Capture is a persistent risk.

**Public benefit corporation / social enterprise.** Legal forms that embed public benefit requirements into corporate charter. Weaker than cooperative or commons governance in terms of participatory accountability, but more accessible as a transition form and more capitalizeable. The risk: public benefit language without governance accountability becomes greenwashing.

**Municipal commons.** Local government as commons steward, with genuine community participation in governance rather than public agency discretion. The difference: a municipal park with an advisory committee is not a commons. A municipal park governed by a resident board with real decision authority over use, maintenance, and rules is closer to a commons. The institutional form requires genuine delegation of authority, not consultation.

**Data commons.** Shared data infrastructure governed by contributors and users rather than by the platform that hosts it. The governance challenge is specific to data: data is non-rivalrous (my use does not reduce yours) but the infrastructure to hold it is not, and the value of data is often in aggregation, which creates incentives for enclosure. Data cooperatives, open data standards with governance mechanisms, and platform cooperatives are partial responses.

## Layer Engagement

**Layer 1 — Material Base:** Commons governance is often most directly about material resources — land, water, food, energy, housing. The material commons (shared land, community food infrastructure, cooperative energy systems) is Layer 1 governance applied to the resources that make all other dimensions of agency possible. Control of material commons is control of the conditions of life.

**Layer 2 — Commons & Institutions:** This is the primary layer. The module's entire content is Layer 2 design: what governance structures sustain collective agency and what makes them vulnerable to the three failure modes. The distinction between state provision and self-governance is a Layer 2 distinction. The three governance forms (cooperative, land trust, municipal commons) are Layer 2 institutional designs.

**Layer 3 — Legibility & Metrics:** What does accountability look like in commons governance? Who monitors the resource? How is benefit distribution tracked? How are governance participation rates measured, and what do they reveal? The legibility challenge for commons is specific: commons governance tends to be relationship-based and tacit, which makes it hard to make legible without destroying what makes it work. The design target: legibility that serves governance accountability without converting the commons into an auditable service delivery system.

**Layer 4 — Decision Systems & Authority:** Commons governance is collective decision-making without hierarchy. The collective choice arrangements (Ostrom's third principle) are Layer 4 design: how decisions are made, what quorums are required, how dissent is expressed, how rule changes are adopted. The conflict resolution mechanisms are Layer 4 design. The relationship between commons governance and external authority (states that may not recognize the right to organize; markets that may try to outcompete the commons) is a Layer 4 challenge.

**Layer 5 — Knowledge & Intelligence:** Shared knowledge is a commons in itself — the data commons, the research commons, the knowledge commons of a community's understanding of its own resource. The epistemic coordination module is Layer 5 design for the knowledge commons. The two modules are in explicit relationship: commons governance requires shared situational awareness, and the epistemic workshop model is one way that shared awareness is built and maintained.

**Layer 6 — Human Capacity & Care:** Governance participation requires care capacity. People who are working multiple jobs, managing chronic precarity, or navigating the systemic stress that this module is partly designed to address cannot participate in governance at the level commons governance requires. Care capacity is a prerequisite for commons participation, not an outcome of it. The commons module depends on the care infrastructure module, which depends on the material base module. The layers are not independent.

## Governance Design

**1. Start with explicit boundaries.** Before a commons can govern itself, it must define who the community is, what resource is being governed, and on what terms membership and resource use are held. This requires a founding process — often contentious — that should be treated as the first act of governance, not as a prerequisite that can be handled casually.

**2. Build collective choice arrangements before the resource is secured.** Governance structure precedes resource acquisition, not the other way around. A community that acquires a resource before developing its governance will reproduce existing informal hierarchies in its governance.

**3. Separate resource access from governance participation.** Not everyone who uses a resource needs to govern it; not everyone who governs a resource uses it equally. Design these relationships explicitly. Who has use rights and on what terms? Who has governance rights and under what conditions? Who has both and can they be separated?

**4. Design for the three failure modes.** Anti-enclosure provisions (legal forms, constitutional protections, monitoring for acquisition attempts), diversity requirements (governing against capture by the best-positioned), and explicit governance requirements for external funding (protecting against substitution).

**5. Build conflict resolution capacity.** The commons will generate conflict. The question is whether the conflict is resolved in ways that maintain the community or fracture it. Low-cost, accessible, trusted conflict resolution is a governance investment, not an administrative function.

**6. Monitor for governance capture.** Who is actually attending meetings? Who is actually making decisions? Who has informal influence that exceeds their formal role? Capture begins in the informal distribution of attention and trust before it appears in formal governance. Monitor both.

**7. Create transition governance explicitly.** Every commons must address leadership succession, governance evolution, and the management of growth or contraction. These moments are the most common points of capture and fragmentation. Explicit protocols for transitions — how they are announced, governed, and completed — are not bureaucracy; they are resilience.

**8. Connect to material support without surrendering governance.** Commons governance is materially demanding. It requires time from participants who may not have time to spare. External funding (state, foundation, market) can provide material support, but funding relationships that give funders governance rights over the commons destroy the commons while maintaining its name. Explicit governance protection in funding agreements is a design requirement.

**9. Recognize and learn from failure.** Commons governance fails. Resource degradation, governance capture, community fragmentation, enclosure — all of these happen. The governance response is not to pretend failure didn't occur but to analyze it, document it, and integrate the analysis into future design. Failure is informative.

**10. Build inter-commons relationships.** Individual commons are vulnerable to external pressure in ways that networks of commons are less vulnerable. Relationships between commons — shared protocols, mutual aid in governance challenges, advocacy coalitions, federated structures — extend the resilience of individual commons without requiring merger or hierarchy.

## What Remains Open

**Scale and heterogeneity.** Commons governance works well in communities with sufficient shared interest, shared context, and shared accountability. It becomes difficult when the community is large, heterogeneous, and geographically dispersed. Federated commons models — networks of smaller commons with explicit inter-commons protocols — are one direction. They have the right logic but underdeveloped implementation. How federation works without creating a new central authority is not fully resolved.

**The market interface.** Commons must coexist with market economies. They must compete for capital, labor, and participant attention against market alternatives that can offer higher individual returns by externalizing collective costs. The design of commons-market interfaces — how commons participate in markets without being absorbed by market logic — is underdeveloped. Worker cooperatives that compete with investor-owned firms have explored this most extensively; the lessons generalize unevenly.

**Long-term persistence.** Most well-documented successful commons have operated over centuries — the Swiss alpine commons, Japanese fishing commons, Spanish irrigation systems. Contemporary commons governance operates in conditions of rapid change (technological, economic, political) that stress the institutions that enable persistence. How contemporary commons build the institutional memory and cultural transmission that allows century-scale persistence is not well understood.

**Incumbent resistance.** Transition mechanics for displacing concentrated private power are the highest-leverage gap. The module has more to say about building commons in spaces that incumbents have vacated than about building them in spaces that incumbents actively defend.

## Provocations This Module Sits With

**P6: Private authority beyond contestation.** This module is partly a response to that provocation — building commons that provide material security and governance capacity outside the reach of private authority. But the module is also vulnerable to it: commons can be outcompeted in capital markets, subject to legal challenge by incumbents with greater legal resources, and undermined by the political power of those who benefit from enclosure. The module offers architecture, not protection.

**P4: Resilience versus speed.** Commons governance is slow. Genuine collective decision-making takes more time than management decision-making, and that slowness is a feature (decisions are better grounded, more legitimate, more likely to be sustained) and a bug (crises arrive faster than deliberation can respond). The module's response: build explicit crisis protocols that specify when and how rapid decision-making can be invoked, with recovery processes that return to collective governance after the crisis. The protocols must be designed before the crisis, not improvised during it.

**P8: Care capacity inversion.** Commons governance participation requires exactly the time, relational capacity, and sustained attention that current material conditions make scarce. The people most in need of commons governance — those most precarious, most burdened by care obligations, most exhausted by survival conditions — have the least capacity to participate in governing it. The design response (building commons governance around support for participation, not just access to the resource) is necessary but not sufficient in conditions of genuine material scarcity.

**P7: Material base as authority mechanism.** The commons is precisely the design response to this provocation: collective governance of the material conditions that determine who can act. But it is also vulnerable to it: a commons that controls land or food or water is a target for enclosure precisely because controlling those resources is controlling the conditions of life for its community.
