# Autonomy Stack Lexicon

*Stage 1–2 — Foundation + Extension*
*Last updated: 2026-03-29*

---

## About This Document

This lexicon defines the vocabulary the Autonomy Stack uses consistently and relies on. It is not a glossary. A glossary defines words. This document maps a terrain — the relationships between concepts matter as much as the definitions themselves.

**Scope.** This document covers ~80 terms drawn from the extraction corpus (5+ appearances), from Stack architecture documents, and from the four Stage 1–2 modules (food-power, care-based-infrastructure, epistemic-coordination, commons-institutional-governance). The Stage 3 module (distributed-resistance) and the Agentic Governance Annex are not yet fully integrated. Terms that are already fully defined as patterns include brief entries here pointing to the pattern document. All other terms receive fuller treatment.

**What this document is not.** It does not resolve conceptual disputes. Where terms are contested or used inconsistently, this lexicon names the contested versions and distinguishes between them. It does not lock vocabulary permanently. See the semantic audit protocol at the end.

**The provocation this document carries.** A lexicon can calcify language at precisely the moment language is contested terrain. Whoever controls definitions can narrow what is thinkable before any formal decision is made. This document includes a semantic audit protocol for that reason. Use it.

## A

**Agency**

The capacity to act in ways that matter — to make decisions that affect one's own conditions and contribute to collective decisions that affect shared conditions. The Autonomy Stack treats agency as the central concern of governance: not who holds power, but whether the conditions under which individual and collective agency is possible are being sustained, improved, or eroded. Agency is neither purely individual nor purely collective; the two dimensions are mutually constitutive and mutually vulnerable.

*Layers:* All | *Related:* Dimensions of Agency, Contestability, Legitimacy

---

**Agricultural Literacy**

Competence not merely in growing food but in understanding the systems through which food is produced, priced, moved, and governed. Distinct from general food literacy (knowing how to cook, or where food comes from) in that it includes market structure, supply chain mechanics, and the political economy of agricultural data. A population that can grow food but cannot read its own supply chain is not agriculturally literate — it is dependent on those who can. Treated in the Food Power module as strategic infrastructure, comparable in civic function to the capacity to read a legal document or interpret a government budget.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Food Power, Systems Literacy, Orientation Scarcity

---

**Automated Bureaucratic Memory**

The use of algorithmic and agentic infrastructure to perform archival, editorial, and continuity functions previously held by human institutions — maintaining organizational memory, tracking decisions, linking related material, enforcing consistency. Not inherently problematic, but introduces a specific accountability gap: when bureaucratic memory is automated, the mechanisms that produced the memory become opaque and resistant to contestation. What the archive contains, what it omits, and how it has been shaped by automated curation are governance questions, not technical ones.

*Layers:* 3, 4 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority | *Related:* Epistemic Legibility, Technical Legibility, Governance of Intelligence

---

**Authority Debt**

The accumulated cost of material neglect — food insecurity, housing instability, care system failure — that eventually exceeds the political cost of addressing it. Authority debt accumulates silently when governance defers maintenance of the material base. When the debt comes due (price spikes, system collapse, care breakdown), it manifests as acute legitimacy crisis. The concept reframes material neglect as a form of borrowing against future political stability, not a neutral saving.

*Layers:* 1, 4 | *Patterns:* Legitimacy Drift, Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Legitimacy, Transactional Governance, Governance by Neglect

---

**Authority Through Integration Capacity**

The principle that in conditions of information abundance, authority derives not from control of information but from the capacity to integrate distributed intelligence into coherent action. Whoever can synthesize, contextualize, and act on distributed knowledge — human and machine — holds structural authority in the current epistemic environment. This reframes the source of authority from information gatekeeping to intelligence integration, and has implications for how governance bodies should be designed (emphasizing synthesis capacity, not information monopoly).

*Layers:* 4, 5 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority, Epistemic Warfare | *Related:* Governance of Intelligence, Orientation Scarcity, Three-Layer Authority Model

## B

**Baseline Risk Absorption**

A governance mechanism in which the state or a commons institution covers survival-level costs — wages, logistics, minimum production stability — so that producers and workers can operate without existential threat. Separates the necessity of survival from the freedom of innovation. Distinct from subsidy (which typically supplements market activity) in that it explicitly isolates a baseline floor below which governance does not allow conditions to fall. Treated in the Food Power module as a precondition for a functioning export industry: you cannot build competitive upside on survival precarity.

*Layers:* 1, 2, 4 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Dual-Track Model, Governance by Neglect, Food Power

## C

**Care Authority**

Legitimacy derived from demonstrated capacity to meet needs, provide support, and maintain relational presence with those who depend on a system. Distinct from enforcement authority (which compels) and administrative authority (which follows procedure). Care authority is earned through action, not conferred by position. It is also harder to contest — a feature that makes it both more effective and more dangerous. See: Moralization Trap, Care-as-Authority.

*Layers:* 4, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Cognitive Authority, Care-as-Authority, Care-as-Support, Enforcement Authority, Moralization Trap

---

**Care Capacity**

The relational and material conditions required for care work to be sustained: time, adequate wages, manageable caseloads, physical safety, psychological support, and governance voice. Not a soft resource. Care capacity is the operational foundation of any care-based system, and it is the resource most systematically depleted by the material conditions that also generate demand for care. Systems that depend on care capacity while destroying the conditions that produce it are not resilient systems — they are extracting a resource without replenishing it.

*Layers:* 1, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Care Authority, Shared Endurance, Failure Management

---

**Care-as-Authority**

Decision-making power exercised within care relationships: who receives which resources, on what terms, in what priority order. Distinct from care-as-support. Must be governed by full contestability standards — transparent criteria, appeal mechanisms, independent oversight — regardless of how caring the decision-maker is. When care-as-authority is not separated from care-as-support, the moralization trap follows: challenging the rules looks like challenging the care.

*Layers:* 4, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion, Institutional Weaponization | *Related:* Care-as-Support, Moralization Trap, Contestability, Care Authority

---

**Care-as-Support**

The relational, direct provision of care: presence, resource connection, emotional attunement, practical assistance. Operates under care ethics rather than administrative procedure. Not less important than care-as-authority — more important in most direct interactions — but governed by different logic. Judging care-as-support by administrative contestability standards misunderstands what makes it effective. Conflating it with care-as-authority is how care systems become uncontestable.

*Layers:* 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Care-as-Authority, Care Authority, Mutual Aid

---

**Cognitive Authority**

Legitimacy derived from localized knowledge and situational awareness, operating independently of official credentials or formal institutional sanction. The person who knows this neighborhood, this community, this specific crisis context holds cognitive authority that no external expert or administrator can substitute for. Central to mutual aid effectiveness and distributed resistance. Not infinitely scalable — cognitive authority degrades as the situation moves beyond direct experience — but irreplaceable in the domains where it operates.

*Layers:* 4, 5 | *Patterns:* Distributed Resistance | *Related:* Care Authority, Shared Situational Awareness, Systems Literacy

---

**Commons**

A resource governed collectively by its users through shared rules, benefit distribution, and accountability structures. Distinct from both state provision (which may lack self-governance) and private ownership (which lacks collective accountability). The commons is not nostalgic or static — it is a living governance form that must be designed for adaptability and democratic control. Threatened by enclosure (conversion to private property), by capture (governance controlled by a subset of users), and by substitution (informal commons replaced by state or market provision without equivalent accountability). A resurgent commons is one that is actively constructed rather than merely defended.

*Layers:* 2 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction, Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Tripartite Governance, State Origin Through Destruction of Commons, Mutual Aid

---

**Compiled Knowledge Infrastructure**

A knowledge-maintenance architecture in which raw sources remain inspectable and stable while agents maintain a structured, cross-referenced synthesis layer from those sources. Distinct from retrieval-only systems: the compiled layer accumulates interpretation, entity pages, concept pages, tensions, contradictions, and links over time. Its governance requirement is strict separation between source claim, interpretive synthesis, and Stack application, so agent-maintained knowledge does not launder interpretation into apparent fact.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Related:* Distributed Epistemic Organism, Epistemic Legibility, Orientation Scarcity, Systems Literacy

---

**Contestability**

The practical capacity to challenge, revise, or reverse a decision, authority structure, or mechanism. The Stack's foundational governance requirement. Not merely formal: contestability that exists in principle but cannot be exercised in practice (due to information barriers, resource requirements, or structural intimidation) is not real contestability. Procedural contestability (can this be challenged now, through this process?) is distinct from substantive contestability (can the decision actually be changed?). Both are required; the latter matters more.

*Layers:* 4 | *Related:* Procedural Contestation, Substantive Contestation, Legitimacy, Moralization Trap, Care-as-Authority

---

## D

**Dark Flight Logistics**

State transport protocols for moving detainees or sensitive populations without public visibility: no airline livery, remote stands, pre-dawn timing, tactical gear. An instance of opacity as operational governance — the deliberate structuring of material logistics to prevent legibility and foreclose contestation. A concrete case of the general principle that material infrastructure is not neutral; how something is moved reveals the governance logic of who is allowed to know and contest that movement.

*Layers:* 1, 3 | *Patterns:* Manufactured Ambiguity, Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Technical Legibility, Epistemic Legibility, Legal Leverage

---

**Dimensions of Agency**

The six layers of the Autonomy Stack, understood not as governance domains but as dimensions of what makes autonomy possible. Each layer describes a distinct aspect of the conditions under which individual and collective agency is possible, sustainable, and contestable: Material Base (what material conditions?), Commons & Institutions (what structures sustain collective agency?), Legibility & Metrics (who controls what can be seen?), Decision Systems & Authority (how are binding decisions made and contested?), Knowledge & Intelligence (how does knowledge form and remain open?), Human Capacity & Care (what relational conditions make sustained agency possible?). The layers are interdependent: stress in one reshapes conditions in all others.

*Layers:* All | *Related:* Agency, Legitimacy

---

**Distributed Epistemic Organism**

A coordination model where participants function as nodes of a single sensing and reasoning system, aggregating distributed human and machine intelligence to produce coherent understanding without centralized control. The governance challenge: distributed epistemic systems can hold more information and process more complexity than centralized ones, but they require explicit protocols for verification, integration, and authority to avoid collapsing into noise or fragmenting under adversarial pressure. See: Three-Layer Authority Model.

*Layers:* 4, 5 | *Patterns:* Epistemic Warfare, Distributed Resistance | *Related:* Harmony, Governance of Intelligence, Shared Situational Awareness, Orientation Scarcity

---

**Dual-Track Model**

A governance architecture that formally separates systems with incompatible mandates — domestic security and export competition in food systems; or more broadly, survival guarantee and market innovation in any essential sector — into distinct tracks with distinct governance, funding, and accountability. The core insight: systems fail when assigned incompatible mandates, and the market logic of the export track actively undermines the stability requirements of the domestic track. The domestic track (baseline guarantee) enables the export track (competitive upside); they are not in tension when properly separated.

*Layers:* 1, 2, 4 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Baseline Risk Absorption, Governance by Neglect, Food Power

---

## E

**Enforcement Authority**

Legitimacy derived from the capacity to compel compliance through threat of sanction — legal, physical, or economic. The dominant authority type in most formal governance systems. Distinct from care authority (which derives from meeting needs) and cognitive authority (which derives from localized knowledge). Not illegitimate per se, but the Stack treats enforcement authority as requiring higher contestability standards than other authority types precisely because it operates through coercion.

*Layers:* 4 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion, Institutional Weaponization | *Related:* Care Authority, Cognitive Authority, Contestability

---

**Epistemic Legibility**

The capacity of those affected by a decision or mechanism to meaningfully understand and challenge it — not just to see it, but to comprehend it well enough to contest it. Distinct from technical legibility. A system can be technically legible (you can see the mechanism) while being epistemically illegible (you cannot understand it well enough to challenge it). Algorithmic decision systems are the canonical example: their outputs are visible while their reasoning is inaccessible. Epistemic legibility requires not just transparency but the human capacity to interpret and respond.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority, Epistemic Warfare | *Related:* Technical Legibility, Legibility, Systems Literacy

---

**Epistemic War**

A conflict framework where shared reality — the collective capacity to distinguish true from false and to coordinate on the basis of shared perception — is the primary contested terrain. Distinct from information warfare (which focuses on the content of messages) in that it targets the conditions under which meaning can be established at all: verification capacity, shared factual baselines, the vocabulary of public reasoning. Epistemic war succeeds not by winning arguments but by making coherent argument impossible.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Epistemic Warfare, Manufactured Ambiguity | *Related:* Shared Factual Baseline, Semantic Capture, Distributed Epistemic Organism

---

## F

**Failure Management**

A governance logic that treats essential systems as designed to manage failure without collapse — not to prevent failure or guarantee outcomes. Hospitals manage illness and death. Fire departments manage fire and loss. Food systems manage supply disruption and price volatility. Systems governed as failure management systems are evaluated on whether they hold under stress, acknowledge constraint honestly, and maintain the conditions for collective endurance through loss. Systems governed as delivery systems are evaluated on outcomes — and become permanently inadequate when conditions exceed their capacity to deliver. See: Shared Endurance, Transactional Governance.

*Layers:* 1, 4, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Shared Endurance, Transactional Governance, Care-as-Support, Care Capacity

---

**Food Power**

The degree to which a political community's capacity to feed its own population translates into political autonomy, trade leverage, and strategic independence. Distinct from food security (which concerns survival) in that it frames domestic provisioning as a source of geopolitical agency, not merely a welfare baseline. Countries that feed themselves reliably gain freedom of action in trade relationships; export systems built on domestic stability are structurally stronger than those built on scarcity and dependency. See: stack/modules/food-power.md.

*Layers:* 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Agricultural Literacy, Dual-Track Model, Baseline Risk Absorption, Governance by Neglect

## G

**Governance by Neglect**

A state in which governance rules — who absorbs risk, who captures benefit — exist implicitly through default social arrangements rather than intentional design. Workers absorb precarity by default. The public absorbs health costs by default. Communities absorb infrastructure failure by default. Governance by neglect is not the absence of governance; it is a specific governance choice whose political advantage is deniability. It directly violates the principle that power without legibility is unacceptable, because the power operating through neglect is invisible as power.

*Layers:* 1, 4 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty, Legitimacy Drift | *Related:* Authority Debt, Dual-Track Model, Baseline Risk Absorption, Transactional Governance

---

**Governance of Intelligence**

A regulatory and design framework for coordinating human and machine cognition: how distributed intelligence is integrated, verified, and acted on without collapsing into noise or concentrating into authoritarian control. Emerges from the observation that in conditions of information abundance, the scarce and contested resource is not information but the capacity to integrate it coherently. Governance of intelligence asks not who controls the data but who controls the synthesis — and whether that control is contestable.

*Layers:* 4, 5 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority, Epistemic Warfare | *Related:* Three-Layer Authority Model, Distributed Epistemic Organism, Automated Bureaucratic Memory, Authority Through Integration Capacity

---

**Grievable Life**

A framework, drawn from Judith Butler, identifying how systems of justice protect only certain populations by defining which lives are worthy of mourning and legal recourse. Which deaths generate institutional response? Which losses are named and acknowledged? The distribution of grievability is a governance choice that precedes and shapes all formal legal protections. In the Stack's frame: care infrastructure that does not acknowledge whose lives it treats as grievable is not providing care — it is providing selective maintenance.

*Layers:* 4, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion, Hybrid War | *Related:* Care-as-Support, Failure Management, Legitimacy

---

## H

**Harmony**

Alignment without uniformity. A coordination principle for distributed systems in which local nodes retain autonomy while shared protocols maintain coherence. Conflict within the system generates information rather than fracture. Distinct from consensus (which requires agreement) and from command (which requires obedience). In distributed resistance and epistemic coordination contexts, harmony is what allows a network to be resilient without being rigid — each node can adapt locally while the overall system remains coherent.

*Layers:* 2, 4, 5 | *Patterns:* Distributed Resistance | *Related:* Distributed Epistemic Organism, Mutual Aid, Three-Layer Authority Model

## I

**Inversion of Law**

A mechanism by which legal frameworks are repurposed to enforce political control rather than protect collective agency — where the language of legality is claimed by those committing violations, and law becomes a tool to fragment solidarity rather than restrain power. Distinct from mere illegality in that the form of law is maintained while its function is reversed. The Stack's framing: inversion of law is a Layer 4 failure that actively undermines every other layer's capacity to function.

*Layers:* 4 | *Patterns:* Institutional Weaponization, Hybrid War | *Related:* Legitimacy, Contestability, State Origin Through Destruction of Commons

---

## L

**Legal Leverage**

A tactical strategy that exploits the time requirements of bureaucratic or legal process to create space for intervention, rather than attempting to stop a process physically. Success is measured in time bought — the window during which legal challenge, public attention, or parallel action can operate. Transforms temporal constraints (bureaucracies need time; lawyers create time) from background conditions into tactical terrain. An instance of how formal governance mechanisms can be used by those they were not designed to serve.

*Layers:* 3, 4 | *Patterns:* Distributed Resistance | *Related:* Contestability, Dark Flight Logistics, Substantive Contestation

---

**Legibility**

The condition of being understandable and contestable by those affected by a mechanism or decision. The Stack distinguishes two types: *technical legibility* (the mechanism can be seen and described) and *epistemic legibility* (it can be meaningfully understood and challenged by those it affects). The Stack's second principle — power without legibility is unacceptable — requires epistemic legibility, not merely technical legibility. A surveillance system is technically legible; it is not epistemically legible to those surveilled. A legal contract in complex technical language is technically legible; it may not be epistemically legible to those who must sign it.

*Layers:* 3 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority, Epistemic Warfare | *Related:* Epistemic Legibility, Technical Legibility, Semantic Capture, Systems Literacy

---

**Legitimacy**

An emergent property of conditions across all Stack layers — not a formal status, not a matter of procedure alone, and not simply a matter of consent. Legitimacy is what happens when authority is exercised in conditions where agency is sustained: when decisions are contestable, when power is legible, when the material base supports participation, when care is not weaponized as dependency. Its absence is a diagnostic signal: when legitimacy collapses, it indicates that one or more dimensions of agency have been foreclosed. Legitimacy cannot be manufactured through communication; it must be earned through conditions.

*Layers:* All | *Patterns:* Legitimacy Drift | *Related:* Contestability, Authority Debt, Transactional Governance

## M

**Metabolic Rift**

A governance failure mechanism, drawn from ecological Marxism, describing the rupture of circular relationships between human systems and the material base — where authority extracts resources without replenishing the ecological cycles that make continued extraction possible. In the Stack's frame: not merely an ecological concept but a governance one. When LA expands into fire-prone hillsides and then cannot contain the fires, this is not a natural disaster but a metabolic rift — decision systems that optimized for property value extraction while destroying the ecological conditions that make habitation viable.

*Layers:* 1, 4 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty, Commons Destruction | *Related:* Governance by Neglect, Authority Debt, Failure Management

---

**Module**

*Stack genre.* An area of the Stack architecture that has been explored in sufficient depth to support structured experimentation — with identified design principles, governance architecture, layer engagement, and named gaps. Modules are not finished products or complete frameworks; they are the level of development at which concrete experimentation becomes possible. Distinguished from patterns (which describe recurring dynamics) and signals (which surface emerging tensions). See: stack/modules/.

*Related:* Pattern, Signal, Provocation

---

**Moralization Trap**

The dynamic in which care-based authority becomes effectively immune to contestation because challenging it appears unkind, ungrateful, or destructive of the only thing that is working. When authority derives from meeting needs, questioning that authority violates social norms of care rather than governance norms of accountability. The moralization trap is not a failure of bad actors; it is a structural feature of any authority system rooted in relational legitimacy. It requires explicit governance design — specifically, the separation of care-as-support from care-as-authority — to prevent care from becoming a new form of uncontestable power.

*Layers:* 4, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Care-as-Authority, Care-as-Support, Contestability, Care Authority

---

**Mutual Aid**

A practice of reciprocal support rooted in collective empowerment, distinguished from charity by its non-hierarchical structure and from state provision by its relational basis. Drawn from Peter Kropotkin's observation that cooperation, not competition, is the primary survival mechanism in complex ecological and social systems. In the Stack's frame: mutual aid is the operational form of care-as-support at collective scale — horizontal, relationship-based, locally governed. Its speed advantage in crises (informal networks scale faster than bureaucracies) is real; its limitation as ongoing care infrastructure (it burns out without structural support) is equally real.

*Layers:* 2, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion, Distributed Resistance | *Related:* Care-as-Support, Commons, Shared Endurance, Tripartite Governance

---

## O

**Orientation Scarcity**

The condition in which information is abundant but the capacity to situate that information within larger systems — to understand context, identify what matters, and act coherently — is scarce. Emerging as the primary epistemic constraint of the current period, as information abundance exceeds the processing capacity of individuals and institutions. The scarce resource is not data but meaning-making: the capacity to ask "what does this mean for what I am trying to do?" in conditions of overwhelming signal. Governance systems that increase information availability without improving orientation capacity are not solving the problem.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Epistemic Warfare | *Related:* Systems Literacy, Governance of Intelligence, Authority Through Integration Capacity, Distributed Epistemic Organism

## P

**Pattern**

*Stack genre.* A recurring governance dynamic that appears across contexts — not a rule, prescription, or unique event, but a recognizable configuration of forces, consequences, and conditions. Patterns describe what tends to happen; they do not prescribe what should happen. They are diagnostic instruments: useful for recognizing a dynamic in a new context, not for predicting outcomes. See: patterns/README.md for current library.

*Related:* Signal, Module, Provocation

---

**Permeable Expertise**

A design condition for systems that require technical expertise while remaining genuinely open to challenge and revision. Distinct from both the elimination of expertise (which produces incompetence) and the insulation of expertise from challenge (which produces technocratic capture). Permeable expertise means: technical knowledge informs design, but design priorities remain democratically governed; challenge is informed rather than merely participatory; some domains distinguish between lay contestation of goals (always appropriate) and lay contestation of technical methods (may require domain knowledge to be effective). There is no clean solution — only better and worse framings of an irreducible tension.

*Layers:* 4, 5 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority | *Related:* Epistemic Legibility, Systems Literacy, Contestability

---

**Private Authority**

Coercive or governing capacity exercised by private actors without formal state status or accountability to democratic processes. Tech platforms that control economic access, private military contractors, corporate infrastructure monopolists. The Stack's current architecture was designed for public governance and has limited mechanisms for contesting private authority. You cannot vote out a platform that controls your market access. The only available mechanisms — exit, disruption, regulation — each carry significant limitations. A recognized gap in the Stack's current reach.

*Layers:* 2, 4 | *Patterns:* Platform Capture, Institutional Weaponization | *Related:* Contestability, Legitimacy, Commons

---

**Procedural Contestation**

The capacity to challenge a decision through formal process while it is being made — to invoke procedure, require deliberation, demand review. Valuable but insufficient as the sole form of contestation. Procedural contestation cannot always operate at the speed that decisions require, and it can be satisfied formally while being emptied substantively. Compare: Substantive Contestation.

*Layers:* 4 | *Related:* Substantive Contestation, Contestability, Legal Leverage

---

**Provocation**

*Stack genre.* An unresolved tension that challenges Stack assumptions — not a problem to be solved but a condition to be navigated. Provocations are explicit about what they challenge in the current architecture and honest about why resolution is difficult. They exist to prevent premature closure on questions the framework cannot yet answer. The eight current provocations are documented in stack/PROVOCATIONS.md.

*Related:* Pattern, Signal, Module

---

## S

**Semantic Capture**

The systematic colonization of governance vocabulary by technical, market, or ideological language — where terms that once named social relationships or political commitments are replaced by terms that name market transactions or technical processes. "Freedom" becomes a market property. "Community" becomes a platform metric. "Security" becomes a surveillance function. Distinct from Epistemic Warfare in targeting vocabulary specifically — the terrain on which contestation occurs — rather than the information environment broadly. Semantic capture narrows what can be thought and argued before any formal decision is made.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Epistemic Warfare, Manufactured Ambiguity | *Related:* Epistemic War, Semantic Circuit Breaker, Inversion of Law

---

**Semantic Circuit Breaker**

A governance mechanism for stopping or pausing decision-making when linguistic disagreement about key terms has reached a threshold where coherent deliberation is no longer possible. An explicit acknowledgment that language is not neutral infrastructure but contested terrain, and that proceeding on the basis of shared vocabulary when vocabulary is not actually shared is not governance — it is the performance of governance. Underdeveloped in current practice; named here as a design target.

*Layers:* 3, 4, 5 | *Related:* Semantic Capture, Epistemic War, Shared Factual Baseline

---

**Shared Endurance**

The fundamental condition that care-based governance provides in place of outcome guarantees: continuity through stress, honest acknowledgment of loss without immediate blame assignment, and the maintenance of relationships that make collective action possible even when the system cannot fix what has broken. Shared endurance is not resilience in the engineering sense (bouncing back) but in the relational sense (remaining in relationship through difficulty). It is what remains when outcomes cannot be guaranteed, and it is what the transactional governance model destroys by making every constraint look like betrayal.

*Layers:* 2, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Failure Management, Transactional Governance, Care Capacity, Mutual Aid

---

**Shared Factual Baseline**

The minimal common ground of facts and reference points that allows deliberation, contestation, and coordination across disagreement. Not consensus — people can disagree fiercely on the basis of shared facts. The shared factual baseline is what makes disagreement productive rather than collapsing it into incompatible realities. Its deliberate destruction is a primary tactic of epistemic war: when there is no shared baseline, every claim is merely the expression of a position, and coordination becomes impossible without coercion.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Epistemic Warfare, Manufactured Ambiguity | *Related:* Epistemic War, Semantic Capture, Distributed Epistemic Organism

---

**Shared Situational Awareness**

Distributed, real-time knowledge of what is happening that allows coordinated action without centralized command. Emerges through informal networks, livestreams, and peer-to-peer communication in crisis and resistance contexts. Distinct from institutional intelligence in that it is not controlled, curated, or delayed by a central authority. The airport resistance example: travelers livestreaming a deportation attempt created shared situational awareness that enabled coordinated intervention without anyone issuing instructions. The governance implication: systems designed to prevent shared situational awareness (opacity, surveillance intimidation) are systems designed to prevent coordination.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Distributed Resistance | *Related:* Cognitive Authority, Distributed Epistemic Organism, Orientation Scarcity

---

**Signal**

*Stack genre.* A short orientation artifact (<200 words) that surfaces emerging tensions, shifts in attention, or early indications of stress or opening — before they are ready to become patterns. Signals do not resolve tension; they name it. Timestamped. Located in /signals/.

*Related:* Pattern, Module, Provocation

---

**State Origin Through Destruction of Commons**

A historical framework, drawn from Silvia Federici, arguing that modern states did not emerge from social contracts but from the destruction of pre-existing commons — the enclosure of shared land, the criminalization of solidarity, the breaking of mutual care networks. The implication for the Stack: the commons is not a nostalgic past form but a living governance alternative that states have historically suppressed because it provided the material and relational basis for collective resistance. Building commons today is not restoration; it is construction under conditions of ongoing suppression.

*Layers:* 1, 2 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction | *Related:* Commons, Mutual Aid, Inversion of Law

---

**Substantive Contestation**

The capacity to actually change a decision, reverse a policy, or restructure a mechanism — not merely to challenge it formally. Distinct from procedural contestation. A system can provide extensive procedural contestation while making substantive contestation structurally impossible: appeals that cannot succeed, reviews that cannot reverse, participation that cannot change outcomes. The Stack's contestability requirement is substantive, not merely procedural. Procedural contestation that cannot produce substantive change is not contestability — it is the performance of accountability.

*Layers:* 4 | *Related:* Procedural Contestation, Contestability, Legal Leverage

---

**System Stewardship**

An institutional role responsible for monitoring system-wide flow, stress, and capacity without owning production assets or directing market decisions. Distinct from central planning (which controls outputs) and regulation (which sets compliance rules). System stewardship asks: is the system as a whole maintaining the capacity to do what it is supposed to do? Who is responsible for that question when no individual actor has jurisdiction over the whole? In food systems, the absence of a steward is the structural reason why grain rots in storage while people go hungry: no one is responsible for the system as a system.

*Layers:* 2, 4 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Dual-Track Model, Governance by Neglect, Failure Management

---

**Systems Literacy**

Competency in understanding how systems — information systems, supply chains, governance structures, ecological cycles — function, propagate, fail, and can be contested. Distinct from factual knowledge (knowing what happened) and ideological alignment (knowing what side you are on). Systems literacy enables the kind of informed contestation that the Stack's principles require: you cannot challenge what you cannot understand, and you cannot understand what you have not learned to read structurally. Treated in the epistemic module as a civic defense infrastructure, comparable to the capacity to navigate legal systems or interpret financial documents.

*Layers:* 3, 5, 6 | *Patterns:* Epistemic Warfare | *Related:* Epistemic Legibility, Agricultural Literacy, Orientation Scarcity

## T

**Technical Legibility**

The condition of being visible and describable — the mechanism can be seen, named, and technically explained. Necessary but insufficient for the Stack's legibility requirement. A surveillance camera is technically legible; it is not epistemically legible to those it watches. An algorithmic scoring system is technically legible to its operators; it is not epistemically legible to those it scores. The Stack's second principle requires epistemic legibility, not merely technical legibility. The distinction matters because technical legibility can be weaponized: making a system visibly complex can be a mechanism for preventing substantive contestation.

*Layers:* 3 | *Patterns:* Algorithmic Authority | *Related:* Epistemic Legibility, Legibility, Automated Bureaucratic Memory

---

**Three-Layer Authority Model**

A governance architecture for multi-agent and distributed intelligence systems, distinguishing three distinct types of authority that must not be collapsed into one: *operational authority* (maintaining infrastructure integrity — keeping systems running), *epistemic authority* (making judgments about the quality and value of knowledge), and *participatory authority* (governing access and contribution). When these are conflated, operational decisions crowd out epistemic judgment; when epistemic authority lacks accountability, it concentrates without checks. Developed in response to the governance challenges of agentic computing systems, but applicable to any distributed intelligence architecture.

*Layers:* 4, 5 | *Related:* Governance of Intelligence, Distributed Epistemic Organism, Authority Through Integration Capacity, Automated Bureaucratic Memory

---

**Transactional Governance**

A governing logic that frames the relationship between citizens and institutions as a vendor relationship: citizens pay (through taxes, through compliance), institutions deliver (specific outcomes), and satisfaction is the measure of legitimacy. The transactional model makes governance systematically illegible under real-world conditions: constraints become betrayals, limitations become evidence of bad faith, and complexity becomes raw material for conspiracy. Distinct from care-based governance not in the quality of intentions but in the governing metaphor that shapes expectations and accountability structures.

*Layers:* 4 | *Patterns:* Legitimacy Drift | *Related:* Failure Management, Shared Endurance, Authority Debt, Governance by Neglect

---

**Tripartite Governance**

A governance architecture distributing authority equally among three constituencies with distinct stakes and knowledge: workers (operational knowledge, bearing the cost of system failure), users (ground-truth experience of whether the system actually works), and community (accountability for impact beyond direct service recipients). Not advisory consultation — actual governance authority. Generates genuine conflict between constituencies with different interests; that conflict is a feature, producing more accurate and legitimate decisions than single-stakeholder governance. Applied in the Care-Based Infrastructure module to essential service governance; appears also in commons, cooperative, and food system governance contexts.

*Layers:* 2, 6 | *Patterns:* Care vs. Coercion | *Related:* Commons, Mutual Aid, Care-as-Authority, Shared Endurance

## Stage 2 Extended Terms

*Terms introduced through the Epistemic Coordination and Commons modules.*

---

**Anti-Enclosure**

Structural protections built into commons governance specifically to prevent conversion to private property or state monopoly. Not merely legal — a commons without political capacity to enforce anti-enclosure provisions is a commons with a declaration, not a protection. Anti-enclosure design includes: legal forms (land trusts, cooperative law, commons licensing) that make enclosure structurally difficult; monitoring for acquisition attempts and early warning systems; political relationships with allied institutions that will contest enclosure; explicit documentation of governance rights that can be invoked in legal proceedings.

*Layers:* 2, 4 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction, Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Enclosure, Commons, Land Trust

---

**Commons Capture**

The takeover of commons governance by a subset of users — typically the most resourced, organized, or politically connected — who redirect the commons for their benefit rather than the whole community. Does not require formal ownership change; requires control of decision-making processes. A cooperative board dominated by its largest members is captured. An ostensibly public institution whose appointments are controlled by a single interest group is captured. Capture often begins in informal power distribution — whose voices are heard in meetings, who has time for governance, who has prior relationships with formal decision-makers.

*Layers:* 2 | *Patterns:* Platform Capture, Institutional Weaponization | *Related:* Commons, Enclosure, Anti-Enclosure, Tripartite Governance

---

**Commons Substitution**

The replacement of a commons by state provision or market provision without equivalent governance accountability — often framed as a more systematic or scalable response. The displacement of mutual aid networks by NGOs that adopt their functions while discarding their governance model is the canonical example. The result: access to the resource is maintained; governance participation is dissolved. Distinct from enclosure in that the resource remains nominally "public" or "nonprofit," but the governance relationship has been severed.

*Layers:* 2, 6 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction, Institutional Weaponization | *Related:* Commons, Mutual Aid, Care Infrastructure Failure

---

**Cooperative**

A business or institution owned and democratically governed by its members — workers, consumers, or some combination. The governance model distributes decision-making rights to members, not to capital investors. The structural challenge: cooperatives are harder to capitalize than investor-owned firms because they cannot offer equity upside, and they make slower decisions because genuine collective governance is slower than management decision-making. Both are features: the capitalization challenge forces reliance on community investment; the decision speed difference reflects the cost of actual participation. See: Tripartite Governance.

*Layers:* 2 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction | *Related:* Commons, Tripartite Governance, Land Trust

---

**Enclosure**

The conversion of a shared resource from commons governance to private property or state monopoly. The term comes from the historical enclosure of shared land in England and Europe, now generalized to any conversion of a governed commons to private or state extraction. Contemporary forms: platform enclosure of digital commons (social networks converting user-generated content into proprietary assets), agricultural enclosure of seed commons (proprietary seed systems replacing open seed-saving), intellectual property enclosure of the knowledge commons. Enclosure is not simply theft; it typically proceeds through legal mechanisms that declare the conversion legitimate. The political fight is often at the level of whether the mechanism is recognized as enclosure at all.

*Layers:* 1, 2 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction, Platform Capture, Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* Commons, Anti-Enclosure, State Origin Through Destruction of Commons

---

**Land Trust**

A governance structure that holds land in permanent community ownership while allowing individuals or organizations permanent use rights. Removes land from speculative markets by legally preventing sale at market rates; use rights (housing, farming, community facilities) can be leased long-term without the land itself being alienable. Established primarily in housing; applicable to agricultural land, urban green space, and community facility ownership. The governance challenge: land trusts require competent stewardship bodies that maintain their public benefit mandate across leadership transitions and resist political pressure to monetize the asset.

*Layers:* 1, 2 | *Patterns:* Commons Destruction | *Related:* Commons, Anti-Enclosure, Cooperative

---

**Propaganda of Technique**

A form of influence, drawn from Jacques Ellul, that is not lies or manipulation but the inevitable output of technical systems ensuring their own continuation. Technical systems generate an environment in which their own solutions appear as the only possible answers to human problems — not because anyone is deceiving anyone, but because participation in the system changes the questions that can be asked within it. The system creates the conditions under which it appears necessary. Distinct from deliberate propaganda in that no actor need intend the effect; it is a structural feature of any sufficiently dominant technical system. The cryptocurrency case is exemplary: the technical design generates the ideology of trustlessness as the natural order.

*Layers:* 3, 5 | *Patterns:* Platform Capture, Epistemic Warfare, Semantic Capture | *Related:* Semantic Capture, Technical Legibility, Epistemic War

---

**Stewardship**

The role of maintaining a shared resource or governance system without owning it — responsible for the conditions that make the resource or system function over time, accountable to those who depend on it, and not entitled to extract value for personal benefit. Distinct from ownership (which confers extraction rights) and management (which typically implies employment authority). Stewardship is the appropriate model for governing commons, public infrastructure, epistemic systems, and any resource whose value is collective rather than individual. The stewardship accountability problem: stewards exercise real authority — they shape what the system can do and who can access it — and that authority must be contestable.

*Layers:* 2, 4 | *Patterns:* Material Base Sovereignty | *Related:* System Stewardship, Commons, Anti-Enclosure, Tripartite Governance

## Tier 2 Pattern Stress-Test Notes

*Stage 2 review: each Tier 2 pattern identified against a concrete case from the recent corpus; provocations named; any strain documented.*

---

**Care vs. Coercion**

*Case:* The mutual aid response to the LA wildfires demonstrates both sides. Mutual aid networks provided care without coercion — relational, fast, responsive to expressed need. Official emergency management deployed coercion-framed-as-care: mandatory evacuation orders, access restrictions, and resource distribution through bureaucratic channels that required compliance documentation. The pattern holds cleanly. The strain: the care module adds the moralization trap as a specific mechanism the pattern understates — genuinely care-based authority can also freeze contestation, not only authority that masquerades as care.

*Provocations engaged:* P2 (care-based authority resisting contestation), P8 (care capacity inversion)

*Status:* Pattern holds. The moralization trap distinction should be added in a future revision as a refinement of "Why It Works."

---

**Platform Capture**

*Case:* The cryptocurrency propaganda analysis demonstrates a variant the pattern understates: capture that operates through the propaganda of technique rather than through deliberate corporate acquisition. The platform does not need to capture governance by force; the technical architecture generates the ideology that makes the platform appear natural and necessary. Crypto platforms concentrate wealth and corporate power while using liberation language — not because operators intend deception but because the technical system generates the justification automatically.

*Provocations engaged:* P1 (legibility masking power — transactional legibility replaces social legibility), P5 (semantic collapse — language colonization replacing social concepts), P6 (private authority beyond contestation)

*Status:* Pattern holds. Strain identified: the pattern emphasizes intentional capture; the propagation-of-technique mechanism should be added as a second pathway.

---

**Obedience in Advance**

*Case:* Civil service adjustments anticipating Schedule F (the reclassification of federal workers) — documented in the early corpus — demonstrate the pattern precisely. Workers and institutions adjusted behavior, moderated communications, and declined to exercise legitimate oversight functions based on anticipated rather than actual directives. The pattern holds without strain.

*Provocations engaged:* P4 (resilience vs. speed — collective resistance requires coordination that OIA makes impossible before resistance can form), P3 (expertise paradox — expert knowledge is suppressed by OIA before it can be formally overridden)

*Status:* Pattern holds without significant strain from recent corpus.

---

**Institutional Weaponization**

*Case:* The civil service "personal loyalty machine" documented in the looming legitimacy crisis analysis — replacing statutory competence with political loyalty, repurposing regulatory oversight against opponents — is the clearest confirmation in the recent corpus. The pattern holds and is deepened by the specific mechanism: the Victory-Legitimacy Paradox cycle, where weaponization generates legitimacy crises for both the weaponizer (who cannot govern without force) and the opposition (who views the system as irreparably compromised). The pattern's current description understates this cycle.

*Provocations engaged:* P6 (private authority — the tech feudal kingmaker model is an instance of Institutional Weaponization applied to private actors), P5 (semantic collapse — "legitimate process" is captured to describe its opposite)

*Status:* Pattern holds. Revision opportunity: add the Victory-Legitimacy Paradox as a downstream consequence of institutional weaponization — it is the crisis that follows successful weaponization, not just a context the pattern operates in.

## Semantic Audit Protocol

*Run this when adding significant new corpus material, when a new module is written, or when terms feel contested in active use.*

**1. Sample.** Select 8–12 terms from this lexicon. Prioritize terms that have been used frequently in recent work, terms introduced in new modules, and terms that the corpus has used in multiple conflicting senses.

**2. Collect uses.** For each selected term, gather 4–6 recent uses from corpus extractions, project documents, or external writing that references the Stack. Use actual quoted sentences, not paraphrases.

**3. Check.** For each use: Is the term being used consistently with its definition here? Are key distinctions (e.g., technical vs. epistemic legibility; care-as-support vs. care-as-authority) being maintained, or are they collapsing? Is the term acquiring new connotations that differ from the intended definition?

**4. Flag.** Terms where usage has drifted significantly fall into three categories:
- *Revise the definition* — if usage has converged on a more precise or useful meaning than the one here
- *Add a disambiguation note* — if the term is genuinely being used in two distinct senses that require separation
- *Flag as potentially captured* — if the term is being pulled toward a meaning that serves different interests than the one the Stack intends

**5. Retire if necessary.** Terms that have become so colonized by other vocabularies that they can no longer carry their intended meaning should be retired — replaced with more precise language — rather than defended with ever-more-elaborate definitions.

**6. Document.** Record what was checked, what was found, what was changed. The audit trail is part of the lexicon's legitimacy.

*Schedule:* Run after each major corpus batch (every ~50 new extractions), after each new module is written, or when active disagreement about a term's meaning surfaces in project work.
