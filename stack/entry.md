# Where to Start

*An orientation for first-time readers.*

---

Something has changed in how governance fails.

The old failure mode was obvious: power concentrated, people resisted, institutions either bent or broke. The architecture of failure was legible even when the failure was catastrophic. You could point to where things went wrong.

The new failure mode is harder to see. Institutions remain in place. Processes continue running. Language that once marked accountability — transparency, participation, oversight, community — is still in use, attached to systems that produce the opposite. You can know something is wrong without being able to say what changed or when it happened. The architecture of failure is opaque even as the failure is experienced directly.

This is what the Autonomy Stack is for.

---

## What the Stack Is

The Stack is a reference architecture for governance in contested conditions. It provides a way to think across systems — food, information, care, institutions, knowledge, relationships — that are normally analyzed and governed in isolation, as if what happened in one had no consequences in another.

It doesn't offer solutions. It offers a frame.

The frame: everything that makes agency possible — individual and collective — can be mapped across six dimensions. Not categories of governance, but dimensions of autonomy itself. Each describes a different kind of question about what makes it possible for people to act together, sustain that capacity, and challenge it when it goes wrong.

The Stack won't tell you what to do. It will help you see where breakdown is occurring, understand how failure in one dimension propagates into others, and identify what kind of problem you are actually facing — which is often different from how it presents.

---

## The Six Dimensions

**Layer 1: Material Base.** What material conditions make agency possible?

Food, energy, housing, logistics. This is where survival meets politics. Agency is harder to sustain when basic material needs are insecure, when the supply of necessities is concentrated in few hands, when the infrastructure people depend on can be withheld. The material base is not just a precondition for everything else — it is a terrain of contest. Control over food and energy is one of the primary mechanisms through which agency is foreclosed without a law being passed or a shot being fired.

**Layer 2: Commons & Institutions.** What structures sustain collective agency — and what makes them vulnerable to capture?

Cooperatives, unions, public agencies, mutual aid networks, standards bodies. This layer asks who takes responsibility for shared resources and what keeps that responsibility from being hollowed out over time. Institutions can be designed well and then captured. They can be inverted — turned against the people they were built to serve. Commons can be enclosed. Coordination can happen without obligation. Understanding how shared structures are built, eroded, and reclaimed is the work of this layer.

**Layer 3: Legibility & Metrics.** Who controls what can be seen — and what can be contested?

What gets measured shapes what gets defended. What goes unmeasured becomes invisible to governance. This layer concerns not only whether systems can be observed but whether they can be meaningfully understood and challenged by those affected. Technical legibility (you can see the output) is not the same as epistemic legibility (you can contest the reasoning). The struggle over what counts as evidence, whose measurements are authoritative, and what vocabulary is used to name a problem — this is a governance struggle before it is a technical one.

**Layer 4: Decision Systems & Authority.** How is agency exercised in binding decisions — and can those decisions be contested, revised, or reversed?

Law, algorithms, administrative procedure, norms. This is where power becomes operational. The primary concern of this layer is not who holds authority but whether the exercise of authority remains open to challenge. Decisions that cannot be undone represent a failure here regardless of who made them. Authority without legitimate process isn't governance — it's force wearing governance's clothing.

**Layer 5: Knowledge & Intelligence.** How does knowledge form, evolve, and remain open to revision?

Research, media, public inquiry, shared situational awareness. Knowledge is not a condition — it is a process. This layer asks whether that process remains genuinely open. Epistemic warfare — the deliberate destruction of shared factual baselines, the fragmentation of shared reality, the colonization of language — is an attack on this process. The challenge isn't only disinformation; it's the degradation of the conditions under which truth and falsehood can be distinguished together at all.

**Layer 6: Human Capacity & Care.** What relational conditions make sustained collective agency possible?

Care networks, mutual support, relational trust, the capacity to remain in sustained engagement over time. This layer is often the last to be analyzed and the first to be depleted. Collective action runs on care capacity. When burnout, precarity, and isolation eliminate that capacity, the other five layers become harder to maintain. Care is not the soft supplement to the real work — it is the substrate on which the real work depends.

## How to Use the Stack

There is no correct entry point.

If you are trying to understand a specific failure — a governance breakdown, a system that isn't working, a situation where agency is being foreclosed — start by asking which layer or layers the breakdown most visibly inhabits. Then ask how it propagates: a failure in Layer 1 (material) creates conditions for Layer 6 (care capacity) depletion; a Layer 3 failure (illegibility) enables Layer 4 capture; Layer 2 erosion (institutional capture) removes the structures through which Layer 5 knowledge becomes actionable.

If you are trying to design something — a governance structure, an institution, a coordination system — move through the layers as a checklist of design questions. Not rules to satisfy but questions to hold: What material conditions does this design require that don't currently exist? Whose interests does the measurement infrastructure serve? How are binding decisions contestable? What happens when it fails?

If you are trying to orient yourself to a situation that is moving too fast — reading it is harder than analyzing it — the [pattern library](../patterns/) may be the more useful entry. Patterns describe the recurring dynamics through which governance breaks down and occasionally holds. Recognizing a pattern in a live situation is often faster than constructing a layer analysis from scratch.

## The Pattern Library

The Stack's patterns are its most immediately applicable artifacts. They describe recurring phenomena — not rules, not solutions, but named dynamics that appear in different domains and scales.

A few that tend to be most applicable first:

**[Legitimacy Drift](../patterns/legitimacy-drift.md)** — Authority abandoning the need for public consent while maintaining its institutional form. The decay is gradual; the moment it becomes visible is usually well after it has become entrenched.

**[Algorithmic Authority](../patterns/algorithmic-authority.md)** — Automated systems accumulating decision-making power by mimicking institutional accountability structures without actually instantiating them. Fast to scale, difficult to contest.

**[Proceduralism as Shield](../patterns/proceduralism-as-shield.md)** — Process deployed as the primary defense against substantive challenge. The accountability mechanism absorbs the challenge without resolving it. Exhausting by design.

**[Care Infrastructure Failure](../patterns/care-infrastructure-failure.md)** — Care systems collapsing not because they were designed wrong but because the material conditions required to make them work have been systematically eliminated. Each burnout looks individual; the cause is structural.

**[Obedience in Advance](../patterns/obedience-in-advance.md)** — Individuals and institutions preemptively constraining their own agency to avoid anticipated pressure. The coercion is rarely explicit; the compliance is real.

The [Pattern Quick Reference](pattern-quick-reference.md) gives all 20 patterns with brief descriptions and layer mappings. The full documents are in [/patterns/](../patterns/).

## The Principles

Eight design principles constrain everything in the Stack. They are not aspirations — they are boundaries that new work must respect:

1. **Authority Must Be Contestable** — If it cannot be meaningfully challenged by those it affects, it isn't legitimate governance.
2. **Power Without Legibility Is Unacceptable** — Making mechanisms visible is a prerequisite, not a solution.
3. **Disagreement Is a Design Condition** — Systems that can only function when everyone agrees are not robust systems.
4. **Resilience Takes Priority Over Elegance** — A system that survives disruption is more valuable than one that optimizes for smooth conditions.
5. **Legibility Takes Priority Over Expertise** — Those affected by decisions must be able to understand and contest them, regardless of technical complexity.
6. **Opaque Mechanisms Require Extraordinary Justification** — Opacity is a governance choice with governance consequences; it needs to earn its place.
7. **Failure Is Informative** — Systems that suppress failure signals in favor of smooth operation are hiding their own breakdown.
8. **Nothing Is Final** — Decisions must remain revisable. Governance that cannot be reversed eventually cannot be governed.

These principles are often locally incompatible — resilience can conflict with speed, contestability can conflict with care-based authority, legibility can conflict with the necessary opacity of some forms of collective action. The Stack does not resolve these tensions. It names them as permanent design conditions. See [PROVOCATIONS.md](PROVOCATIONS.md) for the full inventory of unresolved tensions.

---

## What the Stack Is Not

The Stack is not a solution to any of the problems it describes. It does not provide a blueprint for an alternative system, a theory of change, or a political program. These exist elsewhere and are better developed by people with specific domain expertise, local knowledge, and skin in the game.

The Stack provides a frame for making problems visible across the full range of dimensions that matter for agency. What you do with that visibility is outside the Stack's scope — and appropriately so.

The Stack is also not complete. Its eight unresolved provocations are not gaps to be papered over; they are genuine tensions that the framework has not resolved and may never fully resolve. Before proposing major additions, reading [PROVOCATIONS.md](PROVOCATIONS.md) is essential — many apparent gaps are already named as unresolved tensions requiring framework development, not quick fixes.
